Learn: how things fly

Every flying object is pulled down by gravity. Everything else (air, wind, spin) is a bonus effect. Tap a topic to open it.

1. The basic idea (no air)

Sideways motion and up-and-down motion are separate. Sideways speed stays the same. Gravity changes the up-and-down speed by g every second. The path is a parabola: x = v·cosθ·t and y = h + v·sinθ·t − ½·g·t².

On level ground the range is R = v²·sin(2θ)/g. Examples: the arc of a water fountain jet, or a stone skimmed off a pier.

2. Horizontal launch from a cliff

With no air, the fall time depends only on the height: t = √(2h/g). Throwing harder only carries it farther, not longer. Examples: a ball rolling off a table, water leaving a horizontal pipe, a cannon firing level from a castle wall.

3. Launch height changes the best angle

Launch from higher than the landing level and the best angle drops below 45°: θ = arctan(v / √(v² + 2·g·h)). Examples: in shot put the shot leaves the hand about 2 m above the ground, so the ideal angle is below 45°; athletes often use around 35–40°. A javelin is also thrown low (roughly 30–35°) and its shape adds lift and drag.

4. Landing higher or lower

Landing on a higher level needs a steeper launch, more than 45°. Landing lower needs less. Examples: a basketball free throw is released below the hoop, so a steep arc works best; a ball kicked off a hill lands far below the kicker.

5. Air resistance (drag)

Drag pushes against the motion. Its size is ½·ρ·Cd·A·v², so doubling the speed makes drag four times bigger. Light, wide objects feel drag most. The path becomes steeper on the way down and the range gets shorter. Examples: a cricket ball and a football slow down noticeably; a golf ball has dimples that cut drag; a feather barely travels at all. Cannonballs are heavy, so drag matters less, but real ranges are still shorter than the vacuum formula says.

6. Wind

Drag depends on the speed of the object through the air, so we use the velocity of the object minus the velocity of the wind. A tailwind helps, a headwind hurts and a crosswind pushes the object sideways. Examples: golfers and javelin throwers check the wind before every shot.

7. Spin and the Magnus effect

A spinning ball drags air around with it. The air is pushed to one side, so the ball is pushed the other way. Backspin gives lift, topspin pushes the ball down and sidespin curves it. Examples: a golf driver sends the ball up at a low launch angle (roughly 10–15°) because backspin keeps it airborne, a tennis topspin shot dips quickly, and a curling free kick bends round a wall.

In this lab the lift coefficient is estimated as CL = S / (1 + 2S), where S = r·ω / v. Real balls differ with surface, seams and speed, so treat it as a good teaching model, not an exact match.

8. Altitude

Air gets thinner as you go up (roughly halving every 6 km). Less air means less drag, so objects fly farther at high places. Example: golf balls travel farther on mountain courses. Switch on “Air gets thinner with height” to see this.

9. Gravity on other worlds

For the same launch, range is proportional to 1 / g. The Moon (1.62 m/s²) is about six times weaker than Earth, so a shot goes about six times farther in a vacuum. Example: astronaut Alan Shepard hit golf balls on the Moon during Apollo 14. Jupiter has no solid ground, so the lab treats it as a platform at its cloud level.

10. Space launches

A rocket is not a simple projectile while its engine is firing. After the engine stops, a spacecraft follows a path set by gravity, just like a thrown ball. An orbit is a throw so fast that the ground curves away as fast as the object falls. This lab uses flat ground and constant gravity, so it does not model orbits.

What this lab leaves out

Flat ground, steady wind, no buoyancy (so a very light balloon does not float up), and spin that does not slow down. These keep the physics clear and correct for what is modelled.
